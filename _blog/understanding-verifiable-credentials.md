Verifiable credentials are structured issuer statements with a supported securing mechanism. Understanding them starts by separating the claim, the proof and the verifier's policy decision.

## Three layers to read

The claim describes the subject and achievement. The proof mechanism supports integrity/signing-authority checks. The verifier's policy determines whether the issuer and claim are suitable for the intended use. None of these layers should be silently substituted for another.

## The issuer–holder–verifier workflow

An institution issues a course-completion achievement. The learner presents it to a receiving organization. The receiver checks the supported proof and relevant issuer/status resources, then decides whether the achievement meets its own requirement. Independent identity assurance is a separate process.

## Read the model rather than the marketing shorthand

The [W3C data-model authority guide](/blog/Understanding-W3C-Verifiable-Credentials.html) explains roles and boundaries. It does not require one universal proof mechanism or establish every participant's authority.

## Connect the achievement context

An [Open Badges achievement credential](/blog/why-institutions-should-embrace-open-badges-3-0-standards) describes an achievement within the related standards context. A [Comprehensive Learner Record](/comprehensive-learner-record) can connect multiple achievements with their provenance.

## Prepare the operating responsibilities

The [institutional credential lifecycle](/platform-overview) assigns approval, issuance and maintenance. Ask about export, key/status continuity, access to evidence and supported recipient/verifier behavior before procurement.

## Know the limits

Standards can reduce dependency on a single presentation format, but verification may still require issuer resources. A credential can have a valid proof and an unsupported or inappropriate claim. Do not promise universal interoperability, fraud elimination or privacy merely because a credential is digital.
