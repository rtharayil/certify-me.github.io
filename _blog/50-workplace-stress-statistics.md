Workplace stress statistics help employers understand a problem, but only when the population, date and method stay attached to the number. A US worker survey, a global mental-health estimate and a historical healthcare-cost model answer different questions.

This guide presents figures traceable to named sources, then explains how institutional learning teams can use them without overstating their meaning. Sources were reviewed on 4 October 2026; each figure retains its own reference period.

## Workplace stress statistics with their evidence attached

| Reported figure | Population and period | What the source establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 77% reported work-related stress in the previous month | US employed adults surveyed in April 2023 | APA's *2023 Work in America Survey* describes respondents' experience, not the prevalence among every worker worldwide. |
| 57% reported negative impacts from work-related stress | The same APA 2023 US survey | Respondents reported impacts including exhaustion and reduced motivation. This is not a clinical diagnosis of burnout. |
| An estimated 12 billion working days lost annually | Global depression and anxiety; WHO fact sheet dated 15 September 2026 | A global productivity estimate for those conditions, not workplace stress alone. |
| An estimated US$1 trillion in annual lost productivity | The same WHO global estimate | An economic estimate, not a guaranteed saving available to an employer. |
| An estimated 16% of working-age adults had a mental disorder | Global estimate for 2023, reported by WHO | Working-age adults are not the same population as employed workers; mental disorders are not synonymous with job stress. |
| Up to US$190 billion in annual healthcare costs associated with workplace stressors | Historical US model reported by Stanford in February 2015 | An upper estimate from research by Goh, Pfeffer and Zenios, not a NIOSH measurement or a current-year bill. |

The APA survey was conducted online by The Harris Poll among **2,515 employed US adults, 17–27 April 2023**, with weighting described in the report. Its findings are useful evidence about that surveyed population and period.

The Stanford account describes a meta-analysis examining workplace stressors such as long hours, job insecurity and work–family conflict. A modelled association should not be presented as a directly counted outcome or a precise forecast for another organisation.

## Do not combine figures that measure different things

The WHO productivity estimate and the Stanford healthcare estimate cover different populations, conditions, methods and periods. Adding them would not produce a defensible “total cost of workplace stress.”

Likewise, a percentage reporting stress is not the percentage with a diagnosed disorder. A historical estimate is not current spending. Keep these distinctions in presentations, procurement documents and learning-programme proposals.

## What employers can act on

WHO identifies risks including excessive workloads, low control, insecure work and discrimination. Its guidance covers organisational interventions, manager training, worker mental-health literacy, support and return-to-work arrangements.

Start with the conditions people work in. Training should support an appropriate organisational response rather than substitute for addressing workload, management practice or access to qualified help.

A learning programme can build assessed understanding and recognise completion. Wellbeing outcomes have a separate basis in organisational conditions, qualified support and appropriate outcome evidence.

## Learning within a wider workplace response

Workplace training is one component of a broader response to stress. Its contribution depends on the conditions employees face, the support available and the capabilities being developed. Participation and assessed learning describe the programme; health outcomes describe a different result.

Employee wellbeing information requires appropriate privacy and access arrangements. Organisational changes also influence outcomes, so the effect of training is understood within that wider context.
