Why it matters

A Credential Is More Useful When Its Skills Are Clear

Traditional credentials communicate achievement, but the underlying skills can be difficult for employers, learners and institutions to interpret. A degree tells someone what was studied; a certificate records what was completed. Skills-connected credentials add context about the capabilities that achievement represents.

01 / Achievement

Credential

A degree, certificate, badge, micro-credential or professional certification.

02 / Meaning

Skills

Learning outcomes and competencies represented with structured skill concepts.

From learning to relevance

Move From Credential Data to Workforce Relevance

Structured skills make it possible to examine how achievements relate to occupations and changing workforce requirements. They do not, by themselves, prove a job match or represent live vacancies.

  1. 01

    Credential

    What did the learner or professional earn?

  2. 02

    Learning

    What did they demonstrate or complete?

  3. 03

    Skills

    What capabilities are represented?

  4. 04

    Occupations

    Where may those skills be relevant?

  5. 05

    Workforce demand

    What external evidence supports a particular connection?

Resources such as ESCO's skills and occupations pillars and O*NET's competency frameworks provide context for this work. Institution-specific mappings still require review.

Evidence & standards

Skills Should Be Connected to Evidence

A skill claim is more meaningful when it is related to an achievement issued by an institution. Keep a clear chain between issuer, achievement, evidence, skill and learner record.

W3C Verifiable Credentials describe digitally verifiable claims. Open Badges 3.0 supports achievement and skill claims in portable credentials.
