Trust, with provenance

A learner record is more valuable when someone else can trust it.

A CV says“I have this skill.”
A portfolio says“Here is my work.”
A Skill Passport adds“Here is the evidence, and here is who verified it.”

A learner can add an achievement and supporting evidence, then request an attestation. An educator or mentor reviews the submission and may approve, decline or ask for more information. An approval is digitally signed.

Learner→Achievement / evidence→Attestation→Digital signature→Verified record→Verifier
  1. Record an achievement
  2. Attach supporting evidence
  3. Request an attestation
  4. Educator or mentor reviews
  5. Approve and digitally sign
  6. Add the attested achievement to the record
  7. Share the verification link

A relationship that continues

The institution issues the achievement. The learner carries the record.

The issuing institution remains the trusted source of its credential. The learner maintains a portable record that can grow across institutions, learning and work—rather than ending at graduation.

  1. First achievement
  2. School
  3. University
  4. Internship
  5. Certification
  6. Employment
  7. Lifelong learning

Built once. Grows for life.

Make achievement legible

A credential tells you what was awarded. Skills explain what it represents.

Connecting credentials to structured skills helps learners and institutions describe capability with more context. Mapping can draw on ESCO, O*NET or an institution’s own framework, according to the institution’s approach.

Capability in context

Show what the learner can do, not just what they completed.

Projects connect learning to tangible work. Research, software, design, business initiatives, community activity, competitions, publications and internship work can all add meaningful context to a learner record.
