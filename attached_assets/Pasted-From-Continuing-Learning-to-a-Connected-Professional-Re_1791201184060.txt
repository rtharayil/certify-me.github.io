From Continuing Learning to a Connected Professional Record
How one of Europe's longest-established professional associations connected learning, skills and workforce information
The organisation
One of Europe's longest-established professional associations has supported its profession for generations through continuing education, short-format programmes and structured professional development.
Its relationship with members extends well beyond individual courses. The association provides opportunities for professionals to continue learning, maintain their expertise and develop new capabilities throughout their careers.
As the association's learning portfolio evolved, however, the way those achievements were recorded and connected had become increasingly important.
A member could complete several programmes over a number of years and receive recognition for each one, but those achievements did not necessarily form a connected picture of professional development.
The association wanted to improve this without replacing the systems already supporting its learning and member operations.
The challenge
The starting point was not simply a requirement to issue digital certificates.
The association wanted to understand how a learning achievement could remain useful after the programme had ended.
There were three related questions:
How could the association provide trusted digital evidence of achievement?
How could that achievement communicate the skills represented by the learning?
How could those skills eventually be connected to the wider professional and workforce context?
The project therefore needed to address the complete journey rather than just the credential itself.
This is where the six-layer approach provided the structure for the implementation.
The six-layer approach
Rather than implementing six separate products or projects, the association used the six layers as a way of progressively building value around the same underlying achievement.
1. Presentation & Access
The first requirement was to make the achievement useful and accessible after completion.
Instead of relying only on a certificate document, the association could provide a digital credential with a dedicated credential record, verification access and sharing capability.
This gave members a persistent way to access and present their achievement.
But this was deliberately treated as the first layer, not the end result.
A better-looking certificate alone would not solve the association's broader problem.
2. Standards & Interoperability
The next question was whether the achievement could work beyond the association's own environment.
The credentials were structured using recognised standards, including Open Badges 3.0 and W3C Verifiable Credentials, allowing the achievement to be represented in a way that supports interoperability.
This was important for a professional body whose members may move between employers, professional organisations and learning environments throughout their careers.
The association was therefore not simply creating another proprietary record.
It was creating a standards-based representation of the achievement.
3. Verification & Trust
A professional credential needs to establish more than the fact that a document exists.
It needs to establish who issued it, who earned it and what was actually achieved.
The verification layer provided this foundation.
The member could present the credential, while another party could verify the underlying achievement and its issuer.
This was particularly relevant to a professional association because the value of its recognition depends partly on the trust placed in the organisation itself.
The digital credential therefore carried the association's existing professional authority into a verifiable digital format.
4. Skill Taxonomy Alignment
This was where the project moved beyond conventional digital credentialing.
The association began looking at the learning represented by its programmes in terms of skills and capabilities, rather than only programme names and completion status.
Relevant skills could be associated with credentials and mapped to recognised taxonomies or professional skills frameworks.
This created a common language for describing what a member had developed.
For example, instead of a member's record simply showing:
Completed Professional Programme X

the record could also communicate the capabilities represented by that learning.
This made individual achievements more useful when brought together with other learning experiences.
5. Comprehensive Learner Record
Once credentials and skills could be represented consistently, the next layer was to connect them.
A member's development could no longer be viewed only as a sequence of individual programme completions.
Credentials, skills, competencies and other relevant achievements could contribute to a connected professional record.
This was particularly important for an association whose members may continue professional development over many years.
The value of the record increases as the member continues learning.
A programme completed this year becomes one part of a larger professional history rather than a standalone certificate that sits separately from everything else.
6. Workforce Intelligence & Opportunity
The final layer connected professional development with the environment in which those skills are used.
The skills represented in the professional record could be considered alongside occupations, employers, locations and workforce demand through the workforce intelligence layer.
This created a new relationship between the association's learning portfolio and the professional market.
For the member, it could help provide context around where their capabilities may be relevant.
For the association, it creates a way to consider whether the skills developed through its learning programmes continue to reflect changing professional requirements.
This also creates a feedback loop for future learning:
Workforce demand → Skills → Learning programmes → Member achievement → Professional record → Workforce context
How the six layers came together
The important point was that the six layers were not implemented as six independent initiatives.
They built on one another.
The implementation started with a learning achievement.
That achievement became a digital credential.
The credential was made interoperable and verifiable.
Skills were associated with the achievement.
Those skills could accumulate into a professional record.
The record could then be understood in relation to workforce demand.
The resulting journey was:
Learning
↓  
Achievement
↓  
Presentation & Access
↓  
Standards & Interoperability
↓  
Verification & Trust
↓  
Skill Taxonomy Alignment
↓  
Comprehensive Learner Record
↓  
Workforce Intelligence & Opportunity
That sequence gave the implementation a clear direction.
At every stage, the association could ask a practical question:
What additional value does this layer create for the member and for the organisation?

Implementation
The initial implementation was completed over approximately three months, followed by 30 days of hypercare.
Month 1 — Discovery and design
The project began by understanding the association's existing environment rather than starting with technology configuration.
The teams reviewed the learning and programme processes, member data, completion workflows and relevant integration points.
They also identified the first group of programmes to bring into the digital credential model.
During this stage, the six-layer approach was used to define the target experience—from how an achievement would be presented and verified through to how skills and professional records could eventually be structured.
Month 2 — Integration and configuration
The second month focused on connecting the selected learning workflows to CertifyMe.
Credential structures, branding, achievement information, verification requirements and the relevant skills mappings were configured.
The teams tested the journey from programme completion through credential issuance and member access.
The skills and taxonomy work was developed alongside the credential implementation rather than being treated as a later enhancement.
Month 3 — Pilot and rollout
The third month focused on end-to-end validation.
The association reviewed working versions of the experience through iterative demonstrations.
Feedback was incorporated into subsequent iterations before the selected programmes were moved into production.
This approach allowed the organisation to validate both the technology and the member experience before extending the model further.
30-day hypercare
Following launch, the implementation entered a 30-day hypercare period.
The team supported administrators, monitored the credential workflow and addressed issues arising from real-world programme delivery.
The period also provided an opportunity to refine the operating model before expanding the approach to additional programmes.
What changed
The association started with a familiar model:
Learning → Completion → Certificate
The six-layer approach allowed it to progressively extend that model:
Learning → Achievement → Credential → Verified Skills → Professional Record → Workforce Context
That distinction is important.
The project was not simply about digitising an existing certificate process.
It created a way for the association to connect the learning it delivers, the achievements it recognises, the skills members develop and the professional context in which those skills matter.
For members, this creates a foundation for a professional record that can grow as they continue learning.
For the association, it provides a more structured way to understand and extend the value of its learning portfolio.
And because each layer builds on the previous one, the association can continue expanding the model without having to redesign the entire learning environment each time.
The six-layer approach gave the organisation a practical path from a single learning achievement to a connected professional record—and ultimately to a stronger connection between continuing education and the world of work
